Tail-Likelihood Reinforcement Learning

Shrinivas Ramasubramanian, Daman Arora, Fahim Tajwar, Guanning Zeng, Qingyang Wu, Zhongzhu Zhou, Chenfeng Xu, Haiwen Feng, Yuda Song, Aarti Singh, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, Drew Bagnell, Jeff Schneider†, Andrea Zanette†
Carnegie Mellon University †Joint advising Preprint, 2026
arXiv Code MaxRL (binary rewards)

TL;DR  A continuous reward is a continuum of pass/fail events, one at every threshold. TailRL maximizes the log-likelihood of clearing every level — a critic-free advantage that is one sort over the rollouts you already sample — and matches RLOO's Pass@$1024$ with 8 rollouts instead of $1024$.

A continuous reward decomposes into threshold events. A rollout with reward $r_i$ clears every threshold below $r_i$. Across a group of rollouts these binary outcomes trace the tail-probability $p_\theta(x,\tau)$ at every reward level — the curve whose log TailRL maximizes.

GUI grounding on ScreenSpot-Pro after post-training Qwen2.5-VL-3B (top) and 7B (bottom). All three methods reach similar training accuracy (right), but only TailRL keeps improving with the inference budget: Pass@$1024$ up 7.2–14.6 points, and RLOO's Pass@$1024$ matched with 8 rollouts at 3B and 4 at 7B — 128× and 256× less test-time compute.

Why a Tail Likelihood?

Two policies can share the same expected reward while placing very different probability on high-reward rollouts. The difference appears the moment inference compute is scaled: $\text{Best-of-}k(\theta;x)=\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\le k} r(x,z_i)]$ depends on the upper tail of the reward distribution, not on its mean — and expected-reward training is known to narrow exactly that tail.

The obvious fix, declaring $r=1$ a success and everything below it a failure, throws the information away. Exact success may be unattainable, and lowering the threshold does not help: once a rollout clears it, the objective has no preference for improving further. A continuous reward already defines a success event at every threshold.

The TailRL Objective

For a threshold $\tau\in[0,1)$, the tail-probability $p_\theta(x,\tau) := \Pr_{z\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left(r(x,z)>\tau\right)$ records how often the policy reaches each reward level. Give every level its own independent rollout and require that all of them clear their assigned threshold; the log-probability of passing that audit, per level, is

$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) := \displaystyle\int_0^1 \log p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$

TailRL maximizes the expected log-probability of clearing a uniformly sampled reward threshold. On a binary reward every threshold is the same event, so $p_\theta(x,\tau)=q_\theta(x)$ and TailRL reduces exactly to MaxRL.

Truncating the Mercator series $\log p = -\sum_{k\ge1}(1-p)^k/k$ after $T$ terms gives a compute-indexed family:

$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}^{(T)}(\theta;x) := -\displaystyle\int_0^1\sum_{k=1}^{T}\frac{\left(1-p_\theta(x,\tau)\right)^k}{k}\,d\tau$

  • $T = 1$ recovers expected-reward reinforcement learning
  • $T \to \infty$ recovers the population-level tail likelihood

By the layer-cake identity, every member of this family is exactly a weighted sum of Best-of-$k$ objectives:

Expected Reward

$$\begin{aligned} J_{\mathrm{RL}}(\theta;x) &= \text{Best-of-}1(\theta;x) \\ \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{RL}}(\theta;x) &= \nabla_\theta\,\text{Best-of-}1(\theta;x) \end{aligned}$$

Optimizes Best-of-1

Tail Likelihood

$$\begin{aligned} J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) &= \int_0^1 \log p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau \\ \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\,\nabla_\theta\text{Best-of-}k(\theta;x) \end{aligned}$$

Optimizes a harmonic mixture of Best-of-$k$

The harmonic coefficients $1/k$ fall straight out of the logarithm. They are not chosen or tuned. Expected-reward RL keeps only the first term of this mixture.

The Estimator

Theorem: Unbiased Policy-Gradient Estimator

Given $N$ i.i.d. rollouts $z_1,\ldots,z_N\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ with rewards $r_i:=r(x,z_i)$ and score $S(x,z):=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(z\mid x)$, the estimator

$g^{(N)}_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(x) := \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega(r_i)\,S(x,z_i), \qquad \omega(r_i) := \int_0^{r_i}\frac{d\tau}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{r_j>\tau\}}$

is unbiased for $\nabla_\theta J^{(N)}_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x)$.

Interpretation: the denominator counts the survivors at each level, so a rollout's weight integrates the inverse tail-probability over every level below its reward. A level cleared by one rollout out of $N$ is worth $N$ times a level cleared by all. No critic and no pass-rate estimation, and $N$ rollouts automatically target truncation order $N$.

The denominator changes only when $\tau$ crosses a sampled reward, so the integral is a gap-over-survivors sum over the sorted rewards $r_{(1)}\le\cdots\le r_{(N)}$, with $r_{(0)}:=0$:

$\omega\!\left(r_{(i)}\right) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{r_{(k)}-r_{(k-1)}}{N-k+1}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega\!\left(r_{(i)}\right) = \max_{j} r_j$

The weights are nonnegative and sum to the largest reward in the group, so the best reward observed controls the scale of the update. Mean-centering them within the group gives the advantages used in training.

REINFORCE

$\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N r_i\, S(x,z_i)$

Unbiased for: $\nabla_\theta\,\text{Best-of-}1$

The reward itself is the weight

TailRL (Ours)

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^N \omega(r_i)\, S(x,z_i)$

Unbiased for: $\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k}\nabla_\theta\,\text{Best-of-}k$

The integrated inverse survivor count is the weight

In REINFORCE, increasing $N$ only reduces the variance of a fixed objective. In TailRL, $N$ sets the truncation order: more rollouts move the objective itself toward the tail likelihood. No critic, no extra hyperparameter.

What Changes Relative to GRPO and RLOO

All of these objectives integrate a scalar function of the tail-probability, $J_\phi(\theta;x)=\int_0^1\phi(p_\theta(x,\tau))\,d\tau$. They differ only in the gradient weight $\phi'$ placed on making a reward level more likely.

Objective Gradient weight $\phi'(p)$ Reads as
$J_{\mathrm{RL}}$ (REINFORCE, RLOO) $1$ Every level weighted equally, however rarely it is reached
$J_{\mathrm{GRPO}}$ (population) $1/\sigma_\theta(x)$ One scale per input; flat across levels within it
$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}^{(T)}$ $\dfrac{1-(1-p)^{T}}{p}$ Approaches $1/p$, capped at $T$ for the rarest levels
$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}$ $1/p$ Inverse tail-probability: rare levels dominate

Gradient weight vs. tail-probability. Expected reward is flat; order-$T$ TailRL approaches $1/p_\theta(x,\tau)$ as $T$ grows. GRPO's $1/\sigma$ changes how much an input matters, not which rollouts within it matter.

Experiments

When strong outcomes are common, all three objectives behave similarly. When they are not, the expected-reward baselines fail in one of two ways: they overlook infrequent outcomes, or they retreat to a frequent but less useful one. Four settings isolate these failures. Throughout, $N$ is the training rollout budget and $k$ the inference budget; within each comparison all methods share the same policy, data, optimizer and budget, and differ only in the advantage estimator.

Setting 1 · Exact population objective available

ImageNet Object Localization

Four categorical heads sample a bounding box and the reward is its IoU with the ground truth. The policy is tractable enough to optimize the population objective $J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}$ exactly, giving a reference the finite-rollout estimator can be measured against. From the scalar IoU reward alone, TailRL also matches the L1+GIoU coordinate supervision used by DETR.

Comparison with expected-reward baselines. TailRL at $N\in\{16,64,256,1024\}$ (darker is larger $N$), the exact population objective (dashed), and GRPO and RLOO at $N=1024$. At $N=16$ — $1/64$ the training rollouts — TailRL already exceeds both baselines trained at $N=1024$ on all three metrics.

Setting 2 · Varying the quality of the initial policy

Text-Maze Navigation from Low-Success Initial Policies

A rollout is a token sequence describing a path through a $17\times17$ text maze. Partial credit is given for ending near the goal, but reward $1$ requires a shortest path. Varying supervised pretraining yields initial policies whose shortest-path success rate ranges from about $1\%$ down to $0.01\%$; we post-train each with $N=16$ rollouts per maze.

Pass@$1$ before and after post-training. In the shaded region RLOO ends at zero shortest-path success, and GRPO becomes seed-dependent — some runs learn, others stay at zero, so its mean reflects between-seed failure rather than partial learning. Once successes are common the three methods converge.

TailRL stays reliable when high-reward rollouts are exceptionally rare. It learns from initial policies whose successes are nearly absent, where RLOO fails outright and GRPO becomes seed-dependent.

Setting 3 · Vision-language models at two scales

GUI Grounding with Vision-Language Models

Given a screenshot and an instruction, the VLM emits click coordinates and receives a continuous reward $r(x,z)\in[0,2.5]$ combining proximity, a hit bonus and a format bonus. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL-3B and 7B with $N=8$ rollouts per input and evaluate on ScreenSpot-Pro (figure at top). Training accuracy gives no hint of the separation: all three methods stay within a few points of one another on training batches while their held-out Pass@$k$ and Best-of-$k$ curves diverge increasingly with the inference budget.

TailRL-trained VLMs are better at test-time scaling. The advantage over GRPO and RLOO is not higher training success, but a policy from which good clicks remain recoverable as more samples are drawn.

Setting 4 · A safe but suboptimal shortcut exists

Code Runtime Optimization

Each input is a slow C++ program from the PIE corpus, and the model must write a faster version that stays correct. A rewrite that fails any test earns $0$; otherwise it earns its speedup, measured in gem5 simulated time. Returning the input unchanged is always correct and earns reward $1$ — a dependable shortcut that offers no speedup. We post-train Qwen3-1.7B-Instruct with $N=16$ rollouts per program, three seeds per method.

Initial reward distribution. $74.4\%$ of rollouts are incorrect, $23.5\%$ are correct but no faster than the input (the spike at $1\times$), and only $2.1\%$ are correct and faster.

GRPO and RLOO converge to the shortcut within roughly one hundred steps: correctness above $98\%$, mean reward just below the value of copying, and policy entropy collapsing by one to two orders of magnitude. TailRL keeps producing risky rewrites, trading single-rollout correctness for probability mass on programs that are both correct and meaningfully faster — mean reward $2.92$, nearly three times the copying value, and still rising at the one-epoch budget. On held-out problems its Best-of-$1024$ speedup is $\mathbf{7.7\times}$, against $0.98\times$ for GRPO and $0.96\times$ for RLOO, whose best rollouts never beat the program they reproduce.

(Left) mean reward during one training epoch. (Middle) density of the per-problem Best-of-$1024$ reward on the test set; GRPO and RLOO coincide at $1.0$, i.e. they only echo the input program. (Right) fraction of training rollouts passing every test.

TailRL escapes safe but suboptimal shortcuts. When an easy suboptimal solution exists, expected-reward methods settle for it. TailRL keeps searching and learns to produce rarer and better outcomes.

Both failures share a source. Expected reward credits each reward threshold in proportion to how often the policy reaches it, so the highest rewards contribute almost nothing. TailRL weights each level by the inverse of that frequency — one change, and it addresses both.
