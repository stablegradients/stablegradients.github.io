What is Tail-Likelihood?

Why Tail-Likelihood?

Standard reinforcement learning only maximizes the mean reward, losing coverage over rare excellent outputs. TailRL maximizes the policy's mean reward while simultaneously maximizing its tail-probabilities.

Let's manipulate the reward distribution

Two policies can have the same expected reward and very different odds of producing an exceptional sample. Every preset opens as a matched pair with equal mean reward but different tail probabilities. This difference is reflected in their Best-of-$k$ as we increase sampling.

Tail-Likelihood Reinforcement Learning

Expected-reward RL

$J_{\mathrm{RL}}(\theta;x) := \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\,r(x,z)\,\right]$

Expected reward

TailRL

$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) := \displaystyle\int_0^1 \log p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$

Expected log tail-probability across reward thresholds

TailRL is the expected log tail-probability over uniformly sampled thresholds.

On binary rewards every nontrivial threshold asks the same question, did the rollout succeed, so the integral collapses to a single log-likelihood and TailRL reduces exactly to MaxRL.

The gradients of both objectives are a weighted sum of the score-function $S(x,z) := \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(z \mid x)$.

Expected-reward RL

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{RL}}(\theta;x) = \mathbb{E}_{z}\!\left[\,r(x,z)\,S(x,z)\right]$

Weight: the reward itself

TailRL

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) = \mathbb{E}_{z}\!\left[\left(\displaystyle\int_0^{r(x,z)}\frac{d\tau}{p_\theta(x,\tau)}\right)S(x,z)\right]$

Weight: expected inverse tail-probability

TailRL upweights the gradient of rollouts that are rare and excellent. Where the tail-probability $p_\theta(x,\tau)$ is low, $1/p_\theta(x,\tau)$ is large, so a rollout clearing reward thresholds the policy almost never reaches counts for far more than its reward alone would say.

Aligned with inference time objectives

The gradient of TailRL naturally decomposes as harmonically weighted gradients of Best-of-$k$ objectives.

$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\text{Best-of-}k(\theta;x)-1}{k}$

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\,\nabla_\theta\,\text{Best-of-}k(\theta;x)$

The harmonic weights $1/k$ are derived from the Maclaurin series of the logarithm. They are not selected or tuned. The sum runs over every budget, so TailRL combines Best-of-$k$ objectives across inference budgets instead of committing to one.

A continuous reward already contains that curve

A scalar reward is a stack of success tests. A rollout with reward $r$ passes every threshold below $r$ and fails every threshold above it, so the reward is the height of the stack it clears:

$r(x,z) \;=\; \displaystyle\int_0^1 \mathbf{1}\!\left\{r(x,z) > \tau\right\}d\tau$

Average over rollouts and each test becomes its pass rate, which is exactly the tail probability. So the objective that ordinary RL maximizes is the area under the tail curve:

$\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[r(x,z)\right] \;=\; \displaystyle\int_0^1 p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$

Two policies with very different tail curves can enclose the same area. Expected-reward RL cannot tell them apart.

Easy plug-in for RLVR pipelines

In an existing RL system this is a few lines inside the advantage computation: the same rollout groups as GRPO and RLOO, no critic, and no new hyperparameter. The weights are read straight off the empirical tail probability, because the number of rollouts still surviving a reward threshold is exactly $N\,\hat p_\theta(x,\tau)$. Nothing extra is modelled, fitted or tuned.

Sort the $N$ rewards, divide each reward gap among the rollouts that reach it, mean-center, and use the result as advantages:

$\omega\!\left(r_{(i)}\right) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{r_{(k)}-r_{(k-1)}}{N-k+1}, \qquad A_i = \omega(r_i) - \bar\omega$

Rewards sorted increasingly as $r_{(1)}\le\cdots\le r_{(N)}$, with $r_{(0)}:=0$ and $\bar\omega=\frac{1}{N}\sum_j \omega(r_j)$. The divisor $N-k+1$ counts the rollouts still surviving at that gap.

In a group-based RLVR pipeline, only the advantage computation changes.

Comparing REINFORCE and TailRL advantages

Both methods see identical rollouts and turn them into advantages. REINFORCE promotes a rollout by its raw margin over the group, so its update is spread roughly in proportion to reward. TailRL splits each reward gap among the rollouts that reach it, so a gap only the best rollout clears is worth $N$ times a gap everybody clears.

TailRL moves the sampling-compute frontier

Across every tested model and task, TailRL improves the performance-versus-sampling-compute frontier over expected-reward baselines. The largest gaps appear when exceptional outcomes are rare, or when a safe, moderate shortcut dominates the mean.

ImageNet Object Localization

The task is to draw a bounding box around the object of interest, without classifying it. A rollout is a sampled box and its reward is that box's IoU with the ground truth, so the feedback is continuous rather than pass/fail. The policy is a categorical distribution over a large but finite set of boxes, so the exact reward distribution, and with it the population objective, can be computed rather than estimated.

How good is the population-level objective?

On scalar IoU rewards alone, population TailRL matches or beats supervised objectives that see the ground-truth coordinates directly. It leads L1+GIoU, the strongest of them, on both CorLoc thresholds at comparable mean IoU.

Does the finite estimator approach the exact objective?

Larger training groups move the finite-rollout estimator toward that exact objective, and every TailRL budget stays above the expected-reward baselines.

Task details

A ResNet-50 backbone feeds four categorical heads that sample a bounding box centre, width and height. The reward is the box's IoU with the ground truth. Because the policy is a tractable categorical distribution over a finite box set, the exact policy-induced reward distribution, and so the population objective, can be computed directly. Metrics are CorLoc@$\delta$, mean IoU and Best-of-$k$ IoU on held-out validation images.

Text-maze navigation

Can RL learn when exceptional outcomes are almost absent?

We vary the initial shortest-path success rate from about $1\%$ to $0.01\%$. TailRL remains reliable in the rare-success regime, where RLOO stays at zero and GRPO becomes seed-dependent.

TailRL separates exactly where the high-reward tail becomes hard to observe.

Task details

Each input is a $17\times17$ maze written as text, and a rollout is a token sequence describing a path. The reward $r(x,z)\in[0,1]$ measures proximity to the goal and path length against the shortest path: a rollout earns reward $1$ only by reaching the goal along a shortest path, an event the paper calls shortest-path success. Rollouts that fail still receive partial credit for ending closer to the goal, and successful ones receive more for shorter paths. Varying how much supervised pretraining on goal-reaching trajectories the policy gets yields initial shortest-path success rates from about $1\%$ down to $0.01\%$; every method then post-trains from the same checkpoint with $N=16$. Evaluation is on a held-out set of $1024$ mazes.

Code runtime optimization

Can the objective escape a safe but useless shortcut?

Copying the input is always correct and earns reward $1$. GRPO and RLOO collapse to this shortcut; TailRL keeps producing risky rewrites and reaches $7.7\times$ Best-of-$1024$ speedup on held-out problems, versus below $1\times$ for both baselines.

Expected reward settles for the common moderate outcome. TailRL preserves the rare behavior needed to improve it.

Task details

Each input is a slow C++ program from the PIE corpus of competitive programming, and the model is asked to write a faster version that stays correct. A rollout is a rewritten program, compiled and run against the problem's test suite: it scores zero if it fails any test, and otherwise scores its speedup over the original. Speedup is measured in gem5 time so that timing noise cannot manufacture an improvement. Qwen3-1.7B-Instruct is post-trained with $N=16$ rollouts per program. A policy that simply reproduces its input passes every test and earns mean reward $1$, which is the shortcut this task is built to expose.

GUI grounding with 3B and 7B vision-language models

Do similar one-sample policies scale differently with more inference?

TailRL and RLOO have similar Pass@$1$, but their curves separate as more samples are drawn. TailRL matches RLOO's Pass@$1024$ with $8$ rollouts at 3B and $4$ at 7B: $128\times$ and $256\times$ less inference sampling.

Single-sample accuracy hides how much useful behavior remains recoverable from the policy.

Task details

Given a screenshot and a natural-language instruction to click something, the vision-language policy emits the coordinates of the click. The reward $r(x,z)\in[0,2.5]$ combines proximity to the target, a bonus for landing inside the target element, and a format bonus. Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B are fine-tuned on the GTA1 grounding corpus with $N=8$ rollouts per input, and the training configuration is identical across TailRL, GRPO and RLOO apart from the advantage estimator. Evaluation is on ScreenSpot-Pro, where Pass@$k$ is the probability that at least one of $k$ rollouts clicks inside the target element and Best-of-$k$ reward is the largest continuous reward among them.

Expected-reward training fails in two ways: it overlooks rare good outcomes, or it collapses to a frequent moderate one. TailRL addresses both by keeping rarely reached reward thresholds influential.

One objective. The same rollout pipeline. A better tail.
