What is the tail likelihood?

Fix an input $x$ and a reward level $\tau$. The tail probability is the chance that one rollout from the policy clears that level:

$p_\theta(x,\tau) \;=\; \Pr_{z\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\,r(x,z) > \tau\,\right]$

Read as a function of $\tau$, this is not one number but a curve: the entire upper tail of the reward distribution the policy induces. Every point on it is a likelihood, the probability of the event this rollout is at least this good. A policy with a thicker tail is more likely to produce an exceptional sample, and it is more likely at every level where that matters.

The tail likelihood asks how probable good outcomes are, level by level, rather than how good outcomes are on average.

A continuous reward already contains that curve

A scalar reward is a stack of success tests. A rollout with reward $r$ passes every level below $r$ and fails every level above it, so the reward is the height of the stack it clears:

$r(x,z) \;=\; \displaystyle\int_0^1 \mathbf{1}\!\left\{r(x,z) > \tau\right\}d\tau$

Average over rollouts and each test becomes its pass rate, which is exactly the tail probability. So the objective that ordinary RL maximizes is the area under the tail curve:

$\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[r(x,z)\right] \;=\; \displaystyle\int_0^1 p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$

Two policies with very different tail curves can enclose the same area. Expected-reward RL cannot tell them apart.

The TailRL objective

Keep the curve instead of its area. TailRL maximizes the average log-likelihood across reward levels:

$J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x) \;=\; \displaystyle\int_0^1 \log p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$

Every reward level contributes its own log-likelihood, and every level counts equally.

The logarithm is what changes the incentives. Because $\log p$ falls away without bound as $p\to 0$, a level the policy almost never reaches cannot be traded for a small gain on a level it already clears. Under the mean, that trade is free.

On binary rewards every nontrivial level asks the same question, did the rollout succeed, so the integral collapses to a single log-likelihood and TailRL reduces exactly to MaxRL. Related project: MaxRL for binary rewards →

Why this is a likelihood, not just a reweighting

Take $L$ equally spaced levels $\tau_\ell=(\ell-1)/L$, draw independent rollouts $z_1,\ldots,z_L\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$, and let $E_L$ be the event that every one clears its assigned level. Independence factorizes the likelihood:

$E_L := \displaystyle\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{L}\left\{r(x,z_\ell)>\tau_\ell\right\}, \qquad \Pr_\theta(E_L\mid x) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} p_\theta(x,\tau_\ell)$

The number of levels sets only the resolution of the audit. Requiring the objective to be invariant to that resolution, while agreeing with the ordinary log-likelihood at $L=1$, fixes the normalization at $1/L$. The result is a Riemann sum, and $L\to\infty$ gives the population objective $\int_0^1 \log p_\theta(x,\tau)\,d\tau$.

The audit defines the population likelihood. It is not a sampling procedure: the finite-rollout estimator used in training does not draw one rollout per level.

TailRL is a mixture of Best-of-$k$ objectives

Truncating the objective at order $T$ produces a family indexed by sampling compute. Its gradient is a harmonic mixture of Best-of-$k$ gradients:

$\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{TailRL}}^{(T)}(\theta;x) \;=\; \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{T}\frac{1}{k}\,\nabla_\theta\,\text{Best-of-}k(\theta;x)$

At $T=1$ this is exactly expected-reward RL: one sample, one gradient. Raising $T$ folds in larger inference budgets, and the full tail likelihood is the limit. The $1/k$ weights fall out of the logarithm. They are not a hyperparameter, and there is nothing to tune.

Training against the tail likelihood is training for the way the model will actually be sampled.

A new objective, not a new training stack

TailRL consumes the same rollout groups as GRPO and RLOO. Sort the $N$ rewards, divide each reward gap among the rollouts that reach it, mean-center, and use the result as advantages:

$\omega\!\left(r_{(i)}\right) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{r_{(k)}-r_{(k-1)}}{N-k+1}, \qquad A_i = \omega(r_i) - \bar\omega$

Rewards sorted increasingly as $r_{(1)}\le\cdots\le r_{(N)}$, with $r_{(0)}:=0$ and $\bar\omega=\frac{1}{N}\sum_j \omega(r_j)$. The divisor $N-k+1$ counts the rollouts still surviving at that gap.

No critic No tail-probability model No new hyperparameter One sort + linear passes

In a group-based RL pipeline, only the advantage computation changes.

Show the estimator theorem

Given $N$ i.i.d. rollouts $z_1,\ldots,z_N\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ with rewards $r_i:=r(x,z_i)$ and score $S(x,z):=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(z\mid x)$, the estimator

$g^{(N)}_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(x) := \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega(r_i)\,S(x,z_i), \qquad \omega(r_i) := \int_0^{r_i}\frac{d\tau}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{r_j>\tau\}}$

is unbiased for $\nabla_\theta J^{(N)}_{\mathrm{TailRL}}(\theta;x)$. The denominator counts the survivors at each level, so a level cleared by one rollout out of $N$ is worth $N$ times a level cleared by all. The integrand is piecewise constant, which is why the weight has the closed form above.

The weights are nonnegative and sum to the largest reward in the group, $\sum_i \omega(r_{(i)}) = \max_j r_j$, so the best reward observed controls the scale of the update. Without centering the estimator targets the order-$N$ objective; with the mean-centering used in training it targets order $N-1$.

REINFORCE and TailRL on the same group

Both methods see identical rollouts and turn them into advantages. REINFORCE promotes a rollout by its raw margin over the group, so its update is spread roughly in proportion to reward. TailRL splits each reward gap among the rollouts that reach it, so a gap only the best rollout clears is worth $N$ times a gap everybody clears.

How the weight grows as a level gets rarer

The per-level weight is the reciprocal of the survivor count, so in the population limit it approaches $1/p_\theta(x,\tau)$: inverse-probability weighting, capped by the truncation order. Expected reward gives every level the same weight of $1$, and GRPO's $1/\sigma(x)$ rescales an entire input's update rather than the levels inside it.

TailRL moves the sampling-compute frontier

Across every tested model and task, TailRL improves the performance-versus-sampling-compute frontier over expected-reward baselines. The largest gaps appear when exceptional outcomes are rare, or when a safe, moderate shortcut dominates the mean.

Does the finite estimator approach the exact objective?

ImageNet localization

The categorical policy is tractable enough to optimize population TailRL exactly. Finite-rollout TailRL approaches that reference as $N$ grows, and TailRL at $N=16$ already exceeds GRPO and RLOO trained with $N=1024$.

Using only scalar IoU rewards, population TailRL also matches or exceeds task-specific coordinate supervision.

Task details

A ResNet-50 backbone feeds four categorical heads that sample a bounding box centre, width and height. The reward is the box's IoU with the ground truth. Because the policy is a tractable categorical distribution over a finite box set, the exact policy-induced reward distribution, and so the population objective, can be computed directly. Metrics are CorLoc@$\delta$, mean IoU and Best-of-$k$ IoU on held-out validation images.

Text-maze navigation

Can the objective escape a safe but useless shortcut?

Code runtime optimization

Copying the input is always correct and earns reward $1$. GRPO and RLOO collapse to this shortcut; TailRL keeps producing risky rewrites and reaches $7.7\times$ Best-of-$1024$ speedup on held-out problems, versus below $1\times$ for both baselines.

Expected reward settles for the common moderate outcome. TailRL preserves the rare behavior needed to improve it.

Do similar one-sample policies scale differently with more inference?

GUI grounding with 3B and 7B vision-language models

TailRL and RLOO have similar Pass@$1$, but their curves separate as more samples are drawn. TailRL matches RLOO's Pass@$1024$ with $8$ rollouts at 3B and $4$ at 7B: $128\times$ and $256\times$ less inference sampling.

Single-sample accuracy hides how much useful behavior remains recoverable from the policy.

Expected-reward training fails in two ways: it overlooks rare good outcomes, or it collapses to a frequent moderate one. TailRL addresses both by keeping rarely reached reward levels influential.

One objective. The same rollout pipeline. A better tail.
